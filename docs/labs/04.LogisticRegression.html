Last week, we learnt how to use qualitative variables in a multiple linear regression model to understand the relationship between independent variables X1 … Xn and the dependent variable Y. Today we will learn to use logistic regression for a binary dependent variable. A logistic regression model is a type of regression analysis used when the dependent variable is binary (e.g., “success/failure” or “0/1”). It models the probability that the outcome occurs (e.g. Y = 1) using a logistic function. This model is commonly used in situations like predicting disease presence (yes/no) or customer churn (stay/leave). The independent variables can be continuous, categorical, or a mix of both. The model’s coefficients are on the log-odds scale and are usually converted into odds ratios, which show how each predictor is associated with the odds of the outcome.

The lecture’s slides can be found here.

Learning objectives

An understanding of, and ability to:

  • Estimate and interpret a logistic regression model

  • Assess the model fit

The application context of a binomial logistic regression model is when the dependent variable under investigation is a binary variable. Usually, a value of 1 for this dependent variable means the event occurs, and 0 otherwise. For example, the dependent variable for this practical is whether a person is a long-distance commuter (i.e. 1), and 0 otherwise.

In this week’s practical, we are going to apply logistic regression analysis in an attempt to answer the following research question:

RESEARCH QUESTION: Who is willing to commute long distances?

The practical is split into two main parts. The first focuses on implementing a binary logistic regression model with R. The second part focuses on the interpretation of the resulting estimates.

5.3.1 Interpreting estimated regression coefficients

Compared to statistical inference in a multiple linear regression model (which you did in Weeks 2 and 3), the interpretation of coefficients (B) and odds ratios (Exp(B)) for the logistic regression model has some similarities and some differences:

As before, we read the p-values of the regression coefficients to assess the significance of the coefficients; for instance, by comparing p-values to the conventional level of significance of 0.05:

  • If the p-value of a coefficient is smaller than 0.05, the coefficient is statistically significant. In this case, you can say that the relationship between an independent variable and the outcome variable is statistically significant.

  • If the p-value of a coefficient is larger than 0.05, the coefficient is not statistically significant. In this case, you can conclude that there is no evidence of an association between the independent variable and the outcome variable (which is not the same as proving there is no association).

What is different is the way we interpret the regression coefficients (B), as we need to read the odds ratios (Exp(B)) from exp(coef(m.glm)). An odds ratio below 1 (a negative B) means lower odds of long-distance commuting than the reference category; an odds ratio above 1 (a positive B) means higher odds; and an odds ratio equal to 1 (B = 0) means no difference.

  • The Intercept (odds = 0.070) is the odds of long-distance commuting for the reference group: a male in a higher professional occupation (nssec=2). This corresponds to a probability of 0.070 / (1 + 0.070) = 6.6%.

  • For the variable sex, the negative coefficient and the odds ratio indicate that the odds of commuting over long distances for a female are 0.448 times those of a male (the reference group), with a confidence interval (CI), or likely range, of 0.38 to 0.53, holding all other variables constant (the socio-economic classification variable). Put differently, being female is associated with 55.2% (1-0.448) lower odds of long-distance commuting.

  • For the variable nssec, the coefficients and odds ratios indicate the following for those whose socio-economic classification is (each compared with the reference category, nssec=2, higher professional occupations):

    • Large employers and higher managers (nssec=1) have 1.317 times the odds of higher professionals, but this is not statistically significant (p = 0.127), so there is no evidence that their odds of long-distance commuting differ from the reference group.

    • Lower managerial and professional occupations (nssec=3) have 0.788 times the odds of long-distance commuting compared to our reference category (nssec=2, higher professional occupations), holding all other variables constant (the sex variable), with a likely range (CI) of 0.63 to 0.99 (p = 0.039). Therefore we can also say that workers in lower managerial and professional occupations have 21.2% (1-0.788) lower odds than higher professional workers of travelling 60km or more for work, for the same gender.

    • Small employers and own account workers (nssec=5) have 0.282 times the odds of the reference category (nssec=2, higher professional occupations), holding the gender variable constant, with a likely range (CI) of 0.18 to 0.42. In other words, their odds of long-distance commuting are 71.8% (1-0.282) lower. This is the smallest odds ratio in the model, but remember that each odds ratio only compares a group with the reference category: to test whether nssec=5 differs significantly from another non-reference group (e.g. nssec=8), we would need to change the reference category and re-run the model.

    • Compared to higher professional occupations (nssec=2), workers in routine occupations (nssec=8) have 0.441 times the odds of travelling 60km or more to work, with a CI of 0.32 to 0.61, when the other variables are held constant. Equivalently, being in a routine occupation is associated with 55.9% (1-0.441) lower odds of long-distance commuting.

Going back to our question of whether higher professionals have a lower probability of long-distance commuting than people in other occupations: the results suggest the opposite. Apart from large employers and higher managers (nssec=1), whose odds are not significantly different, every other occupation group has significantly lower odds of commuting 60km or more than higher professionals, holding gender constant.

  • llh: The log-likelihood of the fitted model.

  • llhNull: The log-likelihood of the null model (without predictors).

  • G2: The likelihood ratio statistic, showing the model’s improvement over the null model.

  • McFadden: McFadden’s pseudo R-squared (a common measure of model fit).

  • r2ML: Maximum likelihood pseudo R-squared, also known as Cox & Snell pseudo R-squared.

  • r2CU: Cragg and Uhler pseudo R-squared, also known as Nagelkerke pseudo R-squared (a rescaled version of Cox & Snell that can reach 1).

In multiple linear regression, R-squared indicates the % of the variance in the dependent variable that is explained by the independent variables. In a logistic regression model, R-squared is not directly applicable. Instead, we use pseudo R-squared measures, such as McFadden’s pseudo R-squared, or Cox & Snell pseudo R-squared to provide an indication of model fit. For an individual-level dataset like the SAR, a value around 0.3 is considered a good fit. Our model’s McFadden pseudo R-squared is only 0.030, so it is not that robust for prediction, but it still describes the association between the variables and the categories. To improve the model, we may need more independent variables, which you could identify based on the related literature or debates on this topic.

5.3.3 Recode Socio-economic status variable and explore commuting differences

This time, we want to know whether “Lower supervisory and technical occupations”, “Semi-routine occupations” and “Routine occupations” are associated with a different (higher or lower) probability of commuting over long distances compared to people in other occupations.

We can use mutate() to create a new column that keeps “Lower supervisory and technical occupations”, “Semi-routine occupations” and “Routine occupations” as they are, and groups the rest as “Other occupations”. Here, by using the SAR in code format, we can make this much easier by using:

sar_df <- sar_df %>% mutate(New_nssec = fct_other(nssec,   keep = c("6", "7", "8"),   other_level = "0" ))

Or by using if_else and %in% in R, we can achieve the same result. %in% is an operator used to test if elements of one vector are present in another. It returns TRUE for elements found and FALSE otherwise.

sar_df <- sar_df %>% mutate(New_nssec = if_else(!nssec %in% c(6,7,8), "0" ,nssec))

Use “Other occupations” (code: 0) as the reference category by relevel(as.factor()) and then create the regression model: glm(New_work_distance~sex + New_nssec, data = sar_df, family= "binomial").

5.3.4 Prediction using fitted regression model

Relating to this week’s lecture, we can write down the fitted model. Note that the numbers below are the odds ratios returned by exp(coef(m.glm)), not the log-odds coefficients shown by summary(m.glm), so they multiply together rather than add up:

Odds of long-distance commuting = 0.070 × (0.448 if female) × (1.317 if nssec1) × (0.788 if nssec3) × (0.602 if nssec4) × (0.282 if nssec5) × (0.529 if nssec6) × (0.524 if nssec7) × (0.441 if nssec8). Note that nssec1 is included even though it is not statistically significant: non-significant terms are still part of the fitted model, and R uses them when making predictions.

Equivalently, we can write the same model on the log-odds scale, using the coefficients (B) from summary(m.glm). On this scale the terms add up, just like in linear regression: log-odds of long-distance commuting = −2.653 − 0.802 × Female + 0.275 × nssec1 − 0.238 × nssec3 − 0.507 × nssec4 − 1.265 × nssec5 − 0.637 × nssec6 − 0.645 × nssec7 − 0.819 × nssec8, where each dummy variable equals 1 if the person belongs to that category and 0 otherwise. For example, for a female in a Lower managerial and professional occupation (nssec=3), the log-odds = −2.653 − 0.802 − 0.238 = −3.693. To turn log-odds into odds, we take the exponential: odds = e−3.693 = 0.0249, which is exactly the same as 0.070 × 0.448 × 0.788 from the odds equation above. Finally, the probability = odds / (1 + odds) = 0.0249 / 1.0249 = 2.43%. Both ways give the same answer; the odds-ratio form is often easier to interpret, while the log-odds form shows how the model is actually estimated.

By using R, you can create the object you would like to predict. Here we create three people; see whether you can interpret their gender and socio-economic classification.

objs <- data.frame(sex=c("1","2","1"),nssec=c("7","3","5"))

Then we can predict by using our model m.glm:

predict(m.glm, objs,type = "response")

0.03561605 0.02428386 0.01949671

So let us look at these three people. For the first, a male classified as Semi-routine occupation in NSSEC, the probability that he travels 60km or more to work is only 3.56%. For the second, a female in a Lower managerial and professional occupation, the probability of long-distance commuting is 2.43%. Now work out the prediction for our last person.
