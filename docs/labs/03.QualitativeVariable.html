Last week, we introduced Multiple Linear Regression (MLR) - a statistical method that models the relationship between a dependent variable and two or more independent variables, allowing researchers to examine how various predictors jointly influence an outcome. By using the following R code, we create and interpret the model:

model <- lm(pct_Very_bad_health ~ pct_No_qualifications + pct_Males + pct_Higher_manager_prof, data = census)

summary(model)

In a regression model, independent/predictor variables could be continuous or categorical (or qualitative). While continuous variables capture quantitative effects, categorical variables provide insights into differences across groups. When we say categorical variables, we normally mean:

  • Nominal Data: categorical data without natural order. E.g. Gender, Colour, Country…

  • Ordinal Data: categorical data with a meaningful order. E.g. Education level, Customer satisfaction, Grade…

By blending continuous and categorical predictors, MLR with categorical variables enhances the model’s ability to reflect real-world complexities and improves interpretability, as it allows analysts to assess how each category or group within an independent variable influences the dependent variable.

For most categorical (especially the nominal) variables, they cannot be included in the regression model directly as a continuous independent variable. Instead, these qualitative independent variables should be included in regression models by using the dummy variable approach, transforming categorical information into a numerical format suitable for regression analysis.

However, R provides a powerful way, by automatively handling this process when the categorical variable is designated as a factor and to be included in the regression model. This makes it much easier for you to use categorical variables in the regression model to assess the effects of categorical groupings on the dependent variable alongside continuous predictors.

4.1.2 Correlation

As before, rather than relying upon an impressionistic view of the strength of the association between two variables, we can measure that association by calculating the relevant correlation coefficients.

To calculate the correlation between categorical data, we first use Chi-squared test to assess the independence between pairs of categorical variables, then we use Cramer’s V to measures the strength of association - the correlation coefficents in R.

Pearson’s chi-squared test (χ2) is a statistical test applied to sets of categorical data to evaluate how likely it is that any observed difference between the sets arose by chance. If the p-value is low (typically < 0.05), it suggests a significant association between the two variables.

chisq.test(frs_data$health,frs_data$happy) 
Warning in chisq.test(frs_data$health, frs_data$happy): Chi-squared
approximation may be incorrect

    Pearson's Chi-squared test

data:  frs_data$health and frs_data$happy
X-squared = 45594, df = 60, p-value < 2.2e-16

If you see a warning message of Chi-squared approximation may be incorrect. This is because some expected frequencies in one or more cells of the cross-tabulation (health * happy) are too low. The df means degrees of freedom and it relates to the size of the table and the number of categories in each variable. The most important message from the output is the estimated p-value, which shows as p-value < 2.2e-16 (2.2 with 16 decimals move to the left, it is a very small number so written in scientific notation). P-value of the chi-squared test is far smaller than 0.05, so we can say the correlation is statistically significant.

Cramér’s V is a measure of association for categorical (nominal or ordinal) data. It ranges from 0 (no association) to 1 (strong association). The main downside of using Cramer’s V is that no information is provided on whether the correlation is positive or negative. This is not a problem if the variable pair includes a nominal variable but represents an information loss if both variables being correlated are ordinal.

Q2. Income inequality by gender and health

What conclusions could be drawn in terms of income inequalities with respect to gender and health status? Also think about the statistical significance of these differences.

The interpretation of this table is again similar to what you have learnt in Week 2, but you may find that R has automatically treated the categorical/qualitative variables sex and health as dummy variables. Each category gets a coefficient except the reference category set by fct_relevel(). That is why the output has sexMale but no sexFemale, and no healthVery Bad.

Each coefficient is therefore interpreted as a difference from the reference category, Female with Very Bad health, holding the other variable constant:

  • The Intercept (£12,116) is the predicted net income per capita for the reference group: females with Very Bad health.

  • The association of sexMale is positive and statistically significant (+£2,091, p < 0.001): men have £2,091 more net income per capita than women with the same health status, indicating a gender gap in income.

  • The associations of healthBad and healthFair are not statistically significant (p = 0.90 and 0.18): there is no evidence that their net income per capita differs from people in Very Bad health.

  • The associations of healthGood and healthVery Good are positive and statistically significant (+£2,766 and +£4,932, both p < 0.001): compared with people in Very Bad health, people in Good and Very Good health have £2,766 and £4,932 more net income per capita respectively, suggesting that income rises with better health.

We then read R-squared and Adjusted R-squared to evaluate the model fit. Both are only around 0.016, meaning sex and health together explain only 1.6% of the variation in Net_inc_pp. The model is therefore poor for prediction, and more variables (e.g. education level, occupation, age) would be needed to explain income better. Even so, the F-test is highly significant (p < 2.2e-16), so the income gaps by gender and health identified above are real, even though they explain only a small part of why incomes differ.

4.3.1 Include the categorical variables into a regression model

We will continue with a very similar regression model fitted in last week that relates Percentages long-term illness (pct_Long_term_sick_or_disabled) to Percentages no-qualification (pct_No_qualifications), Percentage Males (pct_Males) and Percentages Higher Managerial or Professional occupation (pct_Higher_manager_prof).

Decide which region to be set as the baseline category. The principle is that if you want to compare the (average) long term illness outcome of Region A to those of other regions, Region A should be chosen as the baseline category. For example, if you want to compare the (average) long term illness outcome of London to rest of regions in the England and Wales, London should be selected as the baseline category.

Implement the regression model with the categorical variables - Region in our case. R will automatically handle the qualitative variable as dummy variables so you don’t need to concern any of that. But you need to let R knows which category of your qualitative variable is your reference category or the baseline. Here we will use London as our first go. Note: We choose London as the baseline category so the London region will be excluded in the independent variable list.

Therefore, first, we set London as the reference:

df$Region <- fct_relevel(df$Region,  "London")

Q4. Regional differences compared with London

Relating back to this week’s lecture notes, indicate what regions have statistically significant differences in the percentage of long-term illness, compared to London?

First, the Significance and the Coefficient Estimates. The p-values in the last column of the output table show that most of the independent variables are significant predictors of pct_Longterm_sick_or_disabled. All coefficients below are interpreted when the other predictors remain the same.

  • The Intercept (7.62) is the predicted long-term sick/disabled rate for a London district (the reference category) when pct_Males, pct_No_qualifications and pct_Higher_manager_prof are all 0%. Since no real district has 0% males, we do not interpret this value directly; it is the baseline value to which the other coefficients are added when making predictions.

  • Continuous variables. Similar to last week, pct_No_qualifications is significant at the <0.001 level (three asterisks ***), indicating a highly statistically significant relationship. We are slightly less confident about pct_Males, which is significant at the <0.01 level (two asterisks **). Their coefficient estimates tell us that:

    • The association of pct_No_qualifications is positive and statistically significant (+0.25, p < 0.001): each 1% increase in residents with no qualifications is associated with a 0.25% increase in the long-term sick/disabled rate, when the other predictors remain the same.

    • The association of pct_Males is negative and statistically significant (−0.17, p < 0.01): each 1% increase in males is associated with a 0.17% decrease in the long-term sick/disabled rate, when the other predictors remain the same.

    • The association of pct_Higher_manager_prof is not statistically significant (p = 0.72).

  • Region dummy variables. R has created a dummy variable (the items starting with Region) for every region except the reference category, London. Each coefficient therefore shows how much higher (positive) or lower (negative) a region’s long-term sick/disabled rate is compared with London, when the other predictors remain the same:

    • Significant at the <0.001 level (***): RegionEast, RegionNorth East, RegionNorth West and RegionWales. For example, the North East has a 1.16% higher rate than London, and Wales a 1.38% higher rate, while the East has a 0.76% lower rate.

    • Significant at the 0.05 level (*): RegionSouth East and RegionWest Midlands. This is weaker evidence than ***, so they are only mildly statistically significant.

    • Not significant: RegionEast Midlands, RegionSouth West and RegionYorkshire and The Humber, meaning their rates are not significantly different from London’s.

    You can interpret the other regions in the same way using their coefficient estimates.

    Reminder: You cannot tell from this output whether North East and Wales, or any two regions other than London, are significantly different from each other, because each region’s coefficient only compares that region with London. To compare North East and Wales, set either of them as the reference category, using df$Region <- fct_relevel(df$Region, "North East") or df$Region <- fct_relevel(df$Region, "Wales"), and re-run the model.

Last but not least, the Model Fit. R-squared (0.759) and Adjusted R-squared (0.750) are both around 0.75: the model explains 75.9% of the variation in the long-term sick/disabled rate across districts, or 75.0% after adjusting for the number of independent variables. Together, they suggest a strong fit of the model.

Here we use mutate() function in R to make it happen:

df <- df %>%
  mutate(
    New_region_label = fct_collapse(
      Region,
      North = c("North East", "North West", "Yorkshire and The Humber"),
      Midlands = c("East Midlands", "West Midlands"),
      South = c("East", "South East", "South West"),
      London = "London",
      Wales = "Wales"
    )
  )

This code may look a bit complex. You can simply type ?mutate in your console. Now in your right hand Help window, the R studio offers you the explanation of the mutate function. This is a common way you can use R studio to help you learn what the function caate() creates new columns that are functions of existing variables. Therefore, the df %>% mutate() means add a new column into the current dataframe df; the New_region_label in the mutate() function indicates the name of this new column is New_region_label. The right side of the New_region_label = indicates the value we want to assign to the New_region_label in each row.

The right side of New_region_label is

fct_collapse(Region, North = c("North East", "North West", "Yorkshire and The Humber"), Midlands = c("East Midlands", "West Midlands"), South = c("East", "South East", "South West"), London = "London", Wales = "Wales")

By using the code, the fct_collapse() function recodes or group each value in the Region column into one of the broader categories: “London”, “South”, “North”,“Midlands” or “Wales”. Specifically, it takes the existing factor levels in Region and collapses multiple detailed regions into fewer, aggregated groups. For example, regions such as “North East”, “North West”, and “Yorkshire and The Humber” are all grouped under “North”, while “East Midlands” and “West Midlands” are combined into “Midlands”, and so on.

Now we use the same way to examine our new column New_region_label:

4.5 Answers to the Questions

4.5.1 Q1 associations with health status

Using the Chi-squared test and Cramér’s V, Table 1 can be completed as follows:

All four associations are statistically significant (p < 0.001). Age group has the strongest association with health status (Cramér’s V = 0.329), and marital status the weakest (0.229). Remember that Cramér’s V tells us the strength of an association, but not its direction.

4.5.2 Q2: income inequality by gender and health status

  • Gender: men have £2,091 more net income per capita than women with the same health status (p < 0.001), indicating a significant gender gap in income.
  • Health: compared with people in Very Bad health, people in Good and Very Good health have £2,766 and £4,932 more net income per capita respectively (both p < 0.001). The differences for Bad and Fair health are not statistically significant.
  • Model fit: sex and health explain only 1.6% of the variation in income (R² = 0.016), so other factors matter much more. However, the income gaps by gender and health are statistically significant.

4.5.3 Q3: categories of Region

table(df$Region) shows 10 regions with 331 local authority districts in total: East (45), East Midlands (35), London (33), North East (12), North West (39), South East (64), South West (30), Wales (22), West Midlands (30) and Yorkshire and The Humber (21).

4.5.4 Q4: regions that differ significantly from London

Holding the other predictors constant, compared with London:

  • Significantly higher: Wales (+1.38%), North East (+1.16%) and North West (+0.78%), all p < 0.001.
  • Significantly lower: East (−0.76%, p < 0.001), West Midlands (−0.42%, p < 0.05) and South East (−0.33%, p < 0.05).
  • Not significantly different: East Midlands, South West, and Yorkshire and The Humber.

4.5.6 Q5: London vs North, and South vs Wales

  • London vs North: yes. With London as the baseline, the North has a 0.47% higher long-term sick/disabled rate than London (p = 0.012), when the other predictors remain the same.

  • South vs Wales: this cannot be answered from model2, because both South and Wales are compared only with London. To test it, set one of them as the reference category, e.g. df$New_region_label <- fct_relevel(df$New_region_label, "South"), and re-run the model. Doing so shows that Wales has a 1.76% higher rate than the South (p < 0.001).

  • Conclusion: after controlling for sex, qualifications and occupation, model2 tells us how each region compares with London: Wales (+1.25%) and the North (+0.47%) have significantly higher long-term sick/disabled rates than London, while the South (−0.51%) and the Midlands (−0.41%) have significantly lower rates. This suggests clear regional inequalities in health across England and Wales.

    Reminder: these conclusions are all relative to London. Although the coefficients suggest an ordering (e.g. Wales appears higher than the North, and the North higher than the South), model2 cannot tell us whether the differences between two non-London regions are statistically significant. To test any such comparison, the model must be re-built with one of those regions as the reference category, as we did above for South vs Wales.

4.5.7 The written-down model and Q6

The model of the new region label is: pct_Longterm_sick_or_disabled (%) = 9.98+ (-0.1842)* pct_Males (%) + 0.2014 * pct_No_qualifications (%) + (-0.0341) * pct_Higher_manager_prof + (0.4742) * North + (-0.5063) * South + (-0.4103) * Midlands + 1.2518 * Wales, where North, South, Midlands and Wales are dummy variables (1 if the district is in that region, 0 otherwise). London is the reference category, so all four dummies are 0 for London. Note that pct_Higher_manager_prof is not statistically significant at the 0.05 level (p = 0.087).

If pct_Males, pct_No_qualifications and pct_Higher_manager_prof are 49, 23 and 11:

  • Wales (Wales = 1, other dummies = 0): 9.98 + (-0.1842)*49 + 0.2014*23 + (-0.0341)*11 + 1.2518*1
  • London (all dummies = 0): 9.98 + (-0.1842)*49 + 0.2014*23 + (-0.0341)*11
